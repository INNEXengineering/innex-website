## Equipment failure expert witness analysis

When a pressure vessel ruptures, a flange leaks, a pump seal fails, or rotating machinery comes apart, the parties need to know why. An equipment failure expert witness establishes the physical failure mechanism from the evidence, then determines whether the failure traces to design, manufacturing, installation, operation, or maintenance.

Each of those possibilities points toward a different responsible party, so the analysis has to be methodical and documented. It relies on the physical evidence, operating and maintenance records, the applicable codes and standards, and the equipment manufacturer's requirements.

## What we investigate

Where possible, the work begins with a documented inspection of the failed equipment before any further disassembly, followed by a review of design documents, operating data, and inspection and maintenance history. Areas of focus include:

- Pressure vessel and piping failures, including pressure-relief system performance
- Bolted flange joint leaks, gasket failures, and improper assembly
- Centrifugal pump, mechanical seal, and bearing failures
- Rotating machinery failures from misalignment, imbalance, and vibration
- Glass-lined equipment and instrumentation failures
- Lubrication failures and wear
- Weld failures and weld repairs
- Adequacy of maintenance, inspection, and reliability programs

## Mechanical design and reliability training

Paul Neumann, P.E., CMRP, CSP, holds a B.S. in Mechanical Engineering from the University of Illinois. His specialized training includes ASME Section VIII Division 1 and 2 pressure vessel design, bolted joint design, pumps and mechanical seals, lubrication, welding technology, and root cause analysis, as well as vibration analysis to ISO 18436-2 Category II.

As a Certified Maintenance & Reliability Professional (CMRP), he evaluates failures not only as isolated events but in the context of the maintenance and reliability program that was supposed to prevent them. That perspective is often decisive in determining whether a failure was sudden and unforeseeable, or the predictable result of deferred or inadequate maintenance.

His training in vibration analysis and in pumps and mechanical seals is particularly relevant to rotating equipment, where condition monitoring data often shows how a failure developed over weeks or months. When laboratory testing of failed parts is needed, the test plan is built around the specific engineering questions in the case, so the results answer them directly.
