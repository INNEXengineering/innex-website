faqs:
  - q: What does an OSHA competent person do on a construction site?
    a: OSHA defines a competent person as someone capable of identifying existing and predictable hazards who has authority to take prompt corrective measures. The scaffold and excavation standards require competent person inspections (1926.451(f)(3) and 1926.651(k)). Under the fall protection standard, competent persons mainly train workers (1926.503) and act as safety monitors (1926.502(h)), and personal fall arrest systems must be inspected before each use (1926.502(d)(21)). Whether a competent person was designated and performed those duties is often a key issue.
  - q: Who is responsible for safety on a multi-contractor construction site?
    a: Responsibility can rest with the general contractor, subcontractors, and equipment owners, depending on who created the hazard, who controlled the work area, and the terms of the contracts. Site safety plans, daily reports, inspection logs, and subcontract agreements help allocate those responsibilities.
  - q: What evidence matters most after a scaffold or trench collapse?
    a: Photographs and video taken before the scene was altered, competent person inspection records, scaffold tags, soil classification and protective system documentation, daily logs, and witness statements. Physical components such as planks, couplers, and shoring should be preserved where possible.

## Construction accident expert witness services

Construction sites change daily. Scaffolds are erected and modified, excavations deepen, and multiple contractors share the same space with different crews and supervisors. When a fall, collapse, or equipment incident occurs, a construction accident expert witness must reconstruct the conditions at the time and evaluate them against OSHA's construction standards in 29 CFR 1926.

Many of those standards depend on inspections and decisions made by people on site. The analysis therefore looks beyond whether a rule was broken, to who had the knowledge and authority to recognize the hazard and correct it, and whether they did.

## What we investigate

The analysis typically draws on photographs and video, inspection logs, daily reports, contracts, equipment manuals, and witness accounts, and on a site visit where conditions still allow. Areas of focus include:

- Falls from heights, roof edges, floor openings, ladders, and leading edges
- Scaffold erection, inspection, loading, access, and collapse
- Trench and excavation cave-ins, soil classification, and protective systems
- Aerial lift and scissor lift tip-overs, entrapment, and ejection
- Heavy construction equipment operation, maintenance, and struck-by incidents
- Competent person designation and inspection practices
- Site safety plans and coordination among general contractors and subcontractors

## Competent person and boom and scissor lift trainer experience

Paul Neumann, P.E., CMRP, CSP, has completed OSHA construction safety training and is a competent person for scaffolding, fall protection, and excavation. He also holds an OSHA Boom Lift & Scissor Lift: Trainer Certification.

OSHA relies on competent persons to inspect scaffolds and excavations, and to train workers on fall protection and monitor its use on a jobsite. Holding them provides a practical basis for evaluating what a competent person on that site should have recognized, when they should have recognized it, and what corrective action was available. That is a more useful question for the trier of fact than a comparison against the text of the regulations alone.

The same training applies to aerial lift and heavy equipment cases, where the questions often include whether the machine was suitable for the task, whether it was inspected and maintained, and whether the operator was trained and familiarized with that specific model. Each opinion is tied to the OSHA provision or consensus standard that applied when the work was performed.
